Engineer the Search: Google Search Operators for Lead Generation

By Aryan Ahire, Founder — Free Mind Consultancy · Published September 22, 2026 · Part of 100 Days of Marketing Engineering

Google is not just a search engine. Most marketers use it the normal way: type a phrase, scroll the results, move on. A Marketing Engineer uses search operators — a small set of symbols and keywords that narrow Google's index down to exactly what you're looking for. With site:, intitle:, inurl: and exact-match search, you can turn Google into a way to find businesses, decision-makers, directories, job posts and prospects, not just articles.

What a search operator actually is

An operator is a piece of syntax you add to a normal Google search that tells it to restrict or target the results in a specific way — a domain, a part of the URL, a word in the title, an exact phrase, or a file type. You're not searching the open web anymore. You're querying Google's index with a filter, the same way you'd add a WHERE clause to a database query. That's the "engineering" part: the search itself becomes a tool you build, not a sentence you type.

The core operators

OperatorWhat it doesExample
site:Limits results to one domain or subdomainsite:linkedin.com/in "marketing manager" "mumbai"
intitle:Requires the word to appear in the page titleintitle:"hiring" "content marketer"
inurl:Requires the word to appear in the URLinurl:contact "book publisher" india
"exact phrase"Matches the phrase exactly, word for word"looking for a marketing agency"
-excludeRemoves a word from the results"content writer" hiring -jobs.com
filetype:Limits results to a file formatfiletype:pdf "media kit" "book publisher"
ORMatches either term (must be capitalized)"marketing manager" OR "growth manager" mumbai
AROUND(n)Matches two terms within n words of each other"founder" AROUND(3) "coaching institute"

Operators combine. A single query can stack a site:, an intitle: and an exact phrase together, and each one narrows the results further.

The workflow

Define→ Build→ Run→ Verify→ Log
  • Define exactly who you're looking for: role, industry, location, signal (hiring, launching, complaining).
  • Build the query by stacking the operators that match that signal.
  • Run it, and skim the first 2–3 pages — quality drops fast after that.
  • Verify each result is a real, current prospect before you reach out.
  • Log the query itself, not just the leads. A good query is reusable next week.

Practical lead-gen examples

Find decision-makers on LinkedIn

  • site:linkedin.com/in "founder" "coaching institute" india
  • site:linkedin.com/in intitle:"marketing head" "book publishing"

Find businesses that are actively hiring for a role you can replace or support

  • intitle:"we're hiring" "content marketer" -site:linkedin.com
  • inurl:careers "social media manager" india

Find directories and listing pages to get discovered on

  • inurl:directory "book publishers" india
  • intitle:"list of" "marketing agencies" india -site:linkedin.com

Find people expressing exact buying intent

  • "looking for a ghostwriter" -jobs
  • "recommend a marketing agency" india site:reddit.com

Find downloadable proof of budget (RFPs, media kits, pitch decks)

  • filetype:pdf "request for proposal" "marketing services" india

Reading the results correctly

  • Recency matters more than volume. A hiring post or a "looking for" comment from eight months ago is a cold lead. Use Google's Tools → Any time filter to restrict to the past week or month.
  • A match isn't a qualified lead. The operator finds the page. You still verify the person, the role and whether they're still relevant before reaching out.
  • Site-specific search respects that site's own indexing. site:linkedin.com only surfaces what Google has crawled and indexed from LinkedIn, which is a partial and sometimes outdated slice of it — not the same as searching LinkedIn's own search bar.
  • Don't over-narrow. Stacking too many exact phrases and operators at once can return zero results. Start broad, then add one operator at a time.

Use AI to generate operator queries at scale

Once you know the pattern, an AI assistant can generate dozens of operator combinations for different roles, industries and cities in seconds — faster than writing them by hand one at a time.

You are a lead-generation researcher using Google search operators.

My business: [describe it in one sentence]
My ideal customer: [role / industry / location / company size]
Signal I'm looking for: [hiring, launching, complaining, asking for recommendations, etc.]

1. Generate 10 Google search queries using operators (site:, intitle:, inurl:, exact phrase, -exclude, OR, AROUND) that would surface this exact type of lead.
2. Vary the operators used across the 10 queries — don't repeat the same combination.
3. For each query, explain in one line what kind of result it should surface.
4. Flag any query that is too narrow and likely to return zero results.

Use it responsibly

  • You're searching Google's public index, not accessing anything private. That's not the same as scraping a site or bypassing a login.
  • Respect the platform you land on. site:linkedin.com to find a profile is fine; scraping LinkedIn's pages in bulk violates its terms.
  • A person's public post, hiring page or directory listing is a lead source, not permission to spam. Personalize before you reach out.
  • Recheck your queries periodically — Google's index and ranking behavior shift, and a query that worked well last month can return different results today.
